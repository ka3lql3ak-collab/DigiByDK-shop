## Welcome & How This Program Works

This is the exact system I use to build my own glutes, and the same structure I give my coaching clients. It isn't a random list of "booty burner" moves. It's a progressive, periodized program, which means every week builds on the last with one goal: to make your glutes measurably stronger and fuller in eight weeks.

### What you're getting

- **8 weeks, 4 training days per week, 32 programmed workouts.**
- **Four phases:** Foundation (weeks 1–2), Build (weeks 3–4), Strength (weeks 5–6), Peak (week 7) and a Show-Off/Test week (week 8).
- **Three lower-body days** focused on glutes, plus **one upper-body day** so your shape looks balanced and athletic, not just "glutes on a frame".
- A full **exercise library** with setup, execution, coaching cues, common mistakes and swaps for every movement.
- Built-in **tracking**: log every set's weight and reps, see what you did last time, and watch your strength climb on the progress charts.

### What you need

A commercial gym with a barbell, bench, cables, a hip abduction machine, leg press, Smith machine and dumbbells. Every exercise has swaps in the library if your gym is missing something.

### How to read each workout

Every exercise shows **sets × reps**, a **rest time**, and a **target RIR** (reps in reserve, explained in Chapter 4). For example, "4 × 8–10 · 2 min · RIR 2" means four sets of eight to ten reps, two minutes of rest between sets, and each set should end when you could have done about two more good reps.

## Glute Anatomy: Know What You're Building

You can't train a muscle well if you don't know what it does. Your "glutes" are actually three muscles, and each responds to different movements.

### Gluteus maximus

The largest muscle in your body and the one that gives your glutes their size and shape. Its main jobs are **hip extension** (driving your thigh back, like the top of a hip thrust), **external rotation** and, for its upper fibers, **abduction** (moving the leg out to the side).

- **Lower glute max** is hit hardest by hip extension under load: hip thrusts, RDLs, deep squats, lunges and back extensions.
- **Upper glute max** ("the shelf") responds to hip extension *plus* abduction: kickbacks angled out, abduction machine leaning forward, and frog pumps.

### Gluteus medius & minimus

These sit on the side and top of your hip, under and above the glute max. They **abduct** the hip and stabilize your pelvis every time you stand on one leg. Well-developed glute meds give the upper-side "round" look and protect your knees and lower back. Hit them with the abduction machine, banded walks, cable abduction and single-leg work.

### The hamstrings & adductors

These aren't glutes, but they're your glutes' teammates in hip extension. Strong hamstrings make your RDLs and deadlifts stronger, and your adductors (inner thigh) are heavily involved in squats and sumo deadlifts. Training them builds a fuller, more athletic lower body from every angle.

### Why exercise variety matters

No single exercise trains every part of the glutes through every range of motion. This program combines:

| Category | Example | What it does best |
|---|---|---|
| Hip thrust pattern | Barbell Hip Thrust | Maximum tension when the glutes are **shortened** (top of the movement) |
| Hinge pattern | RDL, 45° Back Extension | High tension when the glutes are **stretched** |
| Squat & lunge pattern | Bulgarian Split Squat, Smith Squat | Deep hip flexion under load, plus the quads |
| Abduction | Abduction machine, cable kickback | Glute med/min and upper glute max |

> **The research:** Growing evidence suggests that training muscles at long muscle lengths (the stretched position) is especially effective for growth. That's why deep split squats, RDLs and lunges are non-negotiable in this program, even though hip thrusts get all the hype.

::quiz
Q: Which exercise type puts the most tension on the glutes in a STRETCHED position?
- Hip thrust
* Romanian deadlift
- Frog pumps
E: The bottom of an RDL places the glutes and hamstrings under load at long muscle lengths. Hip thrusts and frog pumps are hardest at the top, when the glutes are shortened.
::

## The Science of Muscle Growth

Here's how muscle actually grows, so you understand why every set in this program matters.

### 1. Mechanical tension is the main driver

Muscle grows when its fibers produce high force under load. The reps that count most are the hard ones near the end of a set, when your muscle fibers are fully recruited and moving slowly against heavy resistance. This is why **effort** matters more than "feeling the burn".

### 2. Volume: how much you do each week

Volume is the number of hard sets per muscle per week. Research consistently shows that about **10–20 hard sets per muscle per week** is a productive range for most people. This program starts at the lower end and builds up:

| Phase | Approx. direct glute sets per week |
|---|---|
| Foundation (wk 1–2) | ~14–16 |
| Build (wk 3–4) | ~18–20 |
| Strength (wk 5–6) | ~18–20, heavier |
| Peak (wk 7) | ~18, heaviest |
| Show-Off (wk 8) | ~14, test & recover |

### 3. Frequency: train it 2–3× per week

Training a muscle at least twice per week tends to beat once a week when weekly volume is equal. That's why your glutes are trained on three separate days.

### 4. Proximity to failure

A set only stimulates growth if it's hard enough. Most of your sets should end with **0–3 reps in reserve**. Sets that stop 5+ reps from failure don't do much for growth.

### 5. Rep ranges: heavy isn't the only way

Muscle grows across a wide range of reps, roughly 5 to 30 per set, *as long as the set is taken close to failure*. Heavy sets (5–8) build strength and are time-efficient. Moderate sets (8–15) are the sweet spot for growth. High-rep sets (15–30) are great for isolation moves where heavy loads aren't practical. You'll use all three.

### 6. Recovery is when growth happens

Training is the stimulus. Growth happens while you recover, which depends on sleep, protein and total calories. Chapters 9 and 10 cover these.

> **Coach's note:** "I want to tone, not grow" is a myth. "Toned" simply means having enough muscle and low enough body fat to see its shape. Building muscle *is* toning.

::quiz
Q: What's the main driver of muscle growth?

- Doing a different workout every day
E: Muscles grow in response to high tension, especially in the last few hard reps of a set. Sweat and constant variety don't build muscle.
::

## Effort: The RIR Scale

RIR means **Reps In Reserve**: how many more good-form reps you could have done when you stopped the set. It's the most useful tool for making sure every set is hard enough without wrecking you.

| RIR | What it feels like | When you'll use it |
|---|---|---|
| 4+ | Easy. Could do many more | Warm-up sets only |
| 3 | Challenging, bar speed still fast | Foundation phase, learning a move |
| 2 | Hard, reps start slowing | Most working sets |
| 1 | Very hard, one rep left | Strength & Peak phases |
| 0 | True failure. Couldn't do another rep | Last set of isolation moves only |

### How to get better at judging RIR

Most beginners underestimate how many reps they have left. Do this once in your first two weeks: on the **last set** of a safe machine exercise (leg press, abduction or hamstring curl), predict your RIR, then keep going until you truly can't. If you predicted 2 but did 6 more, you need to push harder on your working sets.

> **Safety rule:** Never take barbell hip thrusts, RDLs, sumo deadlifts or squats to true failure (RIR 0). Use RIR 1–2 on these. Save failure for machines and isolation exercises where failing is safe.

::quiz
Q: You finish a set of leg press and could have done about 2 more good reps. What's your RIR?
- 0
* 2
- 10
E: Reps In Reserve is the number of quality reps left in the tank. Two more possible reps = RIR 2.
::

## Progressive Overload: The Only Rule That Matters

If you do the same weights for the same reps every week, your body has no reason to change. **Progressive overload** means gradually asking your muscles to do more over time.

### The double progression method

This is the system you'll use for every exercise:

1. Each exercise has a **rep range**, e.g. 8–10.
2. Pick a weight you can do for the **bottom** of the range at the target RIR.
3. Each session, try to add reps with the same weight.
4. When you hit the **top** of the range on all sets, increase the weight next session (usually 5–10 lb for lower-body barbell lifts, 2.5–5 lb for dumbbells and isolation work).
5. The reps drop back to the bottom of the range and you build up again.

### A real example: Barbell Hip Thrust, 3 × 8–10

| Session | Weight | Set 1 | Set 2 | Set 3 | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 135 lb | 8 | 8 | 8 | Keep weight, add reps |
| 2 | 135 lb | 10 | 9 | 8 | Keep weight |
| 3 | 135 lb | 10 | 10 | 10 | ✅ Top of range. Add weight |
| 4 | 145 lb | 8 | 8 | 7 | Keep building |

> **The app does this for you:** In every workout, you'll see what you lifted last time next to each exercise. Your only job is to beat it, by one rep or a few pounds.

### Other ways to progress

When you can't add weight or reps, you can still progress by improving **range of motion** (deeper split squats), **control** (a slower 3-second lowering), **pauses** at the hardest point, or **less momentum**. Better reps are progress too.

### When progress stalls

Everyone stalls eventually. If an exercise hasn't improved for **3 sessions in a row**:

1. Check your sleep, protein and calories first. These cause 80% of stalls.
2. Make sure you're actually reaching the target RIR.
3. Drop the weight by 10%, build back up with perfect form, and you'll usually pass your old best within two to three weeks.

::quiz
Q: Your target is 3 × 10–12 and you hit 12, 12, 12 this session. What do you do next time?
- Keep the same weight forever
* Increase the weight and aim for 10+ reps
- Drop to 1 set
E: You hit the top of the range on all sets, so it's time to add weight. Your reps will drop back toward 10 and you build up again. That's double progression.
::

## Choosing Your Starting Weights

Week 1 is your **calibration week**. Don't guess, and don't let your ego pick the weights.

### The calibration process

1. Start each exercise with a weight you're confident you can lift for the target reps.
2. Do your first working set and rate the RIR honestly.
3. Adjust for the next set using the table below.
4. Log the weight that landed you closest to the target RIR. That's your starting point.

| First set felt like... | Adjust the next set by |
|---|---|
| RIR 5+ (way too easy) | +15–20% |
| RIR 4 | +10% |
| RIR 2–3 (on target) | Keep it |
| RIR 0–1 (too hard) | −10% |
| Form broke down | −15–20% and fix technique |

### Suggested starting points for beginners

These are rough guides. Your calibration sets matter more.

| Exercise | Brand-new lifter | Some experience |
|---|---|---|
| Barbell Hip Thrust | Empty bar (45 lb) | 95–135 lb |
| Romanian Deadlift | 2 × 15 lb DBs | Bar + 10s (65 lb) |
| Bulgarian Split Squat | Bodyweight | 2 × 15–20 lb DBs |
| DB Chest Press | 2 × 10–15 lb | 2 × 20–30 lb |
| Lat Pulldown | 40–50 lb | 70–90 lb |

> **Coach's note:** Starting light is not weakness. It's the reason you'll still be progressing in week 8 while the girl who started too heavy is stuck or hurt.

## Warming Up the Right Way

A good warm-up takes 8–10 minutes and makes your first working set stronger and safer.

### Part 1: General warm-up (5 min)

Incline walk or bike at an easy pace until you're warm and breathing a little harder.

### Part 2: Activation (2–3 min)

- Glute bridges × 15 with a 2-sec squeeze
- Banded lateral walks × 10 steps each way
- Bodyweight RDL (hip hinge) × 10

The goal isn't to fatigue the glutes. It's to "wake up" the mind-muscle connection.

### Part 3: Ramp-up sets for your first big lift

Before your first heavy exercise each day, do 2–4 lighter sets. Use the **Warm-Up Calculator** in the Tools tab to get exact weights.

| Ramp set | % of working weight | Reps |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Empty bar or ~40% | 10 |
| 2 | ~60% | 6 |
| 3 | ~80% | 3 |
| 4 (heavy days only) | ~90% | 1 |

You only need ramp-up sets for your **first** exercise of each movement pattern. By the time you get to your accessories, you're warm.

## Rest, Tempo & the Mind-Muscle Connection

### Rest periods

Rest is part of the set. Cutting rest short to "keep your heart rate up" means lifting less weight and getting less tension, which means less growth.

| Exercise type | Rest |
|---|---|
| Heavy compound (hip thrust, RDL, squat, sumo DL) | 2–3 minutes |
| Unilateral & machine compounds | 90 sec – 2 min |
| Isolation (kickbacks, abduction, curls) | 60–90 sec |

The app's **rest timer starts automatically** when you tap off a set.

### Tempo

Control the lowering phase (about 2–3 seconds) on every rep, pause briefly where the exercise is hardest, then lift with intent. Don't bounce out of the bottom of RDLs and split squats.

### Mind-muscle connection

Studies show that focusing on the target muscle can increase its activation, especially on isolation exercises with lighter loads. On kickbacks, abduction and frog pumps, think about the glute doing the work. On heavy compounds, focus on good technique and pushing hard. The glutes will work.

## Recovery, Soreness & Deloads

### Sleep

Sleep is the most underrated growth tool. Aim for **7–9 hours**. Short sleep reduces strength, increases hunger and impairs muscle recovery. If you only fix one thing outside the gym, fix this.

### Daily steps

Aim for **7,000–10,000 steps** on most days. Walking supports recovery, heart health and fat loss without interfering with your lifting.

### Soreness (DOMS)

Soreness 24–72 hours after training is normal, especially in Weeks 1 and 3 when the program changes. **Soreness is not a measure of a good workout.** You can grow without being sore. If you're so sore you can't train with good form, do a lighter session at the planned volume, go for a walk and prioritize sleep.

### Pain vs. effort

| Normal training discomfort | Stop and reassess |
|---|---|
| Burning in the working muscle | Sharp, stabbing or pinching pain |
| Dull soreness the next day | Pain in a joint (knee, hip, lower back) |
| General fatigue | Pain that gets worse with each rep |
| | Numbness or tingling |

If something hurts, swap the exercise for an alternative from the library. If pain persists for more than a few days, see a physical therapist or doctor.

### Week 8 and deloads

Week 8 reduces your total volume while you test your strength. This works as a partial deload. After you finish the program, take **one easy week** (same exercises, half the sets, RIR 3–4) before starting your next block.

## Nutrition for Glute Growth

You can't build muscle out of thin air. Training is the signal, and food provides the building materials.

### Step 1: Choose your phase

| Your goal | Calories | Expected change |
|---|---|---|
| **Build** (grow glutes, accept a little fat) | Maintenance + 100–300 kcal | Weight up ~0.25–0.5 lb/week |
| **Recomp** (newer lifters with some fat to lose) | Around maintenance | Weight stable, shape improves |
| **Lean out** | Maintenance − 300–500 kcal | Weight down 0.5–1% of body weight/week |

> **Coach's note:** If you're newer to lifting, a recomp at maintenance is usually the sweet spot. You can build muscle and lose some fat at the same time. If you've lifted for 2+ years, a small surplus will grow your glutes faster.

### Step 2: Hit your protein

Aim for **0.7–1.0 g of protein per pound of goal body weight** daily (about 1.6–2.2 g/kg). Spread it over 3–5 meals with at least 25–40 g each.

### Step 3: Eat carbs around training

Carbs fuel hard training. Have a carb + protein meal 1–3 hours before you lift (e.g. rice + chicken, oats + protein, a bagel with eggs) and another within a few hours after.

### Supplements that actually work

| Supplement | Evidence | How to use |
|---|---|---|
| Creatine monohydrate | Strong. Improves strength and muscle gain | 3–5 g daily, any time. May add 1–3 lb of water weight (inside the muscle, which is a good thing) |
| Protein powder | Strong (it's just food) | Use to reach your daily protein |
| Caffeine | Strong for performance | 1–3 mg/kg 30–60 min before training |
| Vitamin D | Useful if deficient | Ask your doctor to test |

## Tracking Your Progress

The mirror changes slowly, and the scale fluctuates for reasons that have nothing to do with fat or muscle. Track these three things instead:

### 1. Strength (the most important)

If your hip thrust, RDL and split squat numbers are going up while your nutrition is consistent, your glutes are growing. Use the **Progress** tab to see your charts.

### 2. Photos

- Take them every 2 weeks, in the same place, same lighting, same time of day (morning, before eating).
- Front, side and back. Relaxed, then flexed.
- Same outfit every time.
- Compare Week 0 to Week 8, not Tuesday to Wednesday.

### 3. Measurements

Measure the widest part of your glutes, your hips, your waist at the navel and the top of your thigh every 2 weeks. Log them in the Progress tab. A growing glute measurement with a stable or shrinking waist is the goal.

### Why the scale lies

Your weight can swing 2–5 lb day to day from water, salt, carbs, digestion, your menstrual cycle and soreness (sore muscles hold water). Look at the **weekly average**, not individual days.

## Troubleshooting: Fix It Fast

**"I feel hip thrusts in my quads."**
Your feet are too close to your body. Move them forward until your shins are vertical at the top. Also make sure you're driving through your heels.

**"I feel hip thrusts in my lower back."**
You're arching at the top instead of finishing with your glutes. Tuck your chin, keep your ribs down and tilt your pelvis under at the top. Reduce the weight if needed.

**"I feel RDLs in my lower back, not my hamstrings."**
You're probably rounding or lowering too far. Stop at the point where your hamstrings are fully stretched. That might be just below the knee, and that's fine. Push your hips back further, not down.

**"The hip thrust bar hurts my hips."**
Use a thick bar pad, or fold a yoga mat under the pad. A Smith machine or hip thrust machine works too.

**"My knee hurts in Bulgarian split squats."**
Move your front foot further forward, lean your torso slightly, and make sure your knee tracks over your toes. If pain persists, swap to reverse lunges or step-ups.

**"I don't feel my glutes in kickbacks."**
Lower the weight, slow down and stop the movement before your back arches. Kick back and slightly out.

**"I missed a workout."**
Don't skip it. Do it next and push the schedule back a day. If you miss a whole week, repeat the previous week.

**"The gym is packed and I can't get the equipment."**
Use the swaps in the exercise library. Same movement pattern, same rep range, same effort.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I do cardio on this program?**
Yes. Walking and incline walking are ideal. Keep hard cardio (sprints, HIIT, spin classes) to 1–2 sessions a week, and not right before leg days.

**Can I add an extra glute day?**
Not in your first run. More isn't better if you can't recover from it. Push harder on the four days you have.

**Will this work at home?**
The program is designed for a gym. With a barbell, bench, bands and adjustable dumbbells, you can use the swaps in the library for most exercises.

**I'm on my period and feel weak. What do I do?**
Train anyway if you feel up to it, but use RIR 3 and don't chase PRs. Strength usually comes back within a few days.

**Is it normal to gain weight at the start?**
Yes. New training increases water and glycogen stored in muscle. A 1–3 lb bump in the first two weeks is normal and is not fat.

**How long until I see results?**
Most women notice strength gains within 2–3 weeks, feel a difference in how their jeans fit by week 4–6, and see a clear change in photos by week 8. Real transformations come from repeating good blocks for 6–12 months.
